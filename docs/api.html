Breaker

struct

A circuit breaker that sheds a fraction of calls rather than all of them.

There is no open, half-open and closed: the breaker keeps a rolling window of outcomes and refuses each new call with a probability that grows with the failures in it. A struggling backend keeps receiving as much as it can still serve, instead of being cut off and then flooded again when it recovers.

With accepts successes out of total calls in the window, failing the run of all-failure buckets at its head and working the run of all-success ones:

w     = max(k - (k - floor) × failing / size, floor)
drop  = (total - protection - w × accepts) / (total + 1)
drop ×= (size - working) / size

A drop at or below zero lets everything through, which is the whole healthy case.

Held by the caller. Its fields are private; the methods below are the way in.

Shedder

struct

Load shedding that refuses work only when the server is both hot and full.

Hot means the CPU is at or over the threshold, or was within the cooling time and the shedder has refused since. Full means more calls are in flight than the server has shown it can finish: the busiest bucket's passes per millisecond times the fastest bucket's mean response time, scaled down as the CPU nears 100%. Both the moving average and the current count of calls in flight must be over that figure.

A CPU spike with nothing queued sheds nothing, and neither does a queue on an idle CPU. CPU use is read by the caller, in thousandths, and passed to each Shedder::allow.

Held by the caller. Its fields are private; the methods below are the way in.

Sliding

struct

A sliding window counter: at most quota events in any window long stretch.

Where a bucket smooths, a window counts. A quota of "a thousand a day" is a window, not a rate, and answering it with a bucket would let a client spend the whole day's allowance in the first minute.

The stamps of the events in the window are kept, so the count is exact rather than the approximation a two-bucket scheme gives.

Period

struct

A fixed window counter: at most quota events per period, the count starting again when a period ends.

Cheaper than Sliding, which keeps every stamp, and coarser: a client can spend one quota at the end of a period and another at the start of the next, so forty events can pass in a second that allows twenty. That is the trade a fixed window makes, and the reason it is the cheap one.

A period opens on the first event after the last one closed. With align it opens on a multiple of period on the caller's clock instead, so with a Unix clock a daily quota resets at midnight UTC rather than a day after first use.

Jitter

enum derives Debug, Eq

How much randomness goes into a delay.

A retry with no jitter makes every client that failed together try again together, which is how one outage becomes a second one. Full, Equal and Decorrelated are the three shapes the literature names and measures; Spread keeps the wait within a fraction of the ceiling instead, which is the easier one to reason about when the schedule is fixed by something else:

Rigidthe computed delay, unchanged. Reproducible, and it synchronises clients
Fulluniform over [0, cap]. The one that spreads clients furthest
Equalhalf the cap plus uniform over [0, cap/2]. Keeps a floor under the wait
Decorrelateduniform over [base, previous × 3]. Grows from where it last landed rather than from the attempt number
Spread(f)uniform over [cap × (1 − f), cap × (1 + f)], f held to [0, 1]. So the wait can pass the cap by f
  • Rigid
  • Full
  • Equal
  • Decorrelated
  • Spread(Double)

07Windows

Window

struct

A rolling window: size buckets that together cover the last window of time, oldest dropping off as time moves on.

The bucket type is the caller's. A breaker keeps a Tally of outcomes per bucket, a shedder a Total of response times; anything with a Default can be a bucket, and the default is what a bucket is cleared back to.

Time never moves on its own: every call carries the now it happens at. Writing at a later now clears the buckets the gap swept past; reading at a later now skips them without clearing, so a window nobody writes to still ages out, and two reads at the same now agree.

Held by the caller. Its fields are private; the methods below are the way in.

08Presets

sre

preset let sre : Sre

The preset: k 1.5 decaying to 1.1, five calls of protection, a probe every second, and ten seconds of history in forty buckets.

k and protection are the slope and the floor of the standard client-side throttling formula; the decay, the probe and the recovery scaling are what keep a long outage from hurting past successes, and a short one from being remembered for long.

shed

preset let shed : Shed

The preset: five seconds in fifty buckets, overloaded at 90% CPU, a one-second assumed response time, an average weighted 0.9, one second to cool and never less than a tenth of capacity.

The memory is short on purpose: a five-second window follows the load it is in rather than averaging a burst away, and the floor keeps a server already at full CPU from being told it can serve nobody.

backoff

preset let backoff : Backoff

The preset: three attempts, a hundred milliseconds doubling to a one-second ceiling, spread by a fifth either way.

Each wait is the computed ceiling spread by a fifth below or above it, so a plan of 0.8 to 1.2 of the schedule is easy to explain to whoever asks why the second retry waited 168 ms and not 200.
